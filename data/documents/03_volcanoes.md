## Overview

A volcano is an opening in the Earth's crust through which molten rock, gas and ash
escape from the interior. Volcanic activity is not distributed randomly across the
globe. Most volcanoes lie along the boundaries of tectonic plates, and the great
majority of those form the belt around the Pacific Ocean commonly called the Ring of
Fire. Understanding volcanoes therefore begins with understanding the movement of the
plates that make up the outer shell of the planet.

The rigid outer layer of the Earth, the lithosphere, is broken into about a dozen major
plates that float on the hotter, ductile asthenosphere beneath. The plates move a few
centimetres a year, driven by convection in the mantle and by the pull of dense oceanic
slabs sinking under their own weight. Where plates diverge, converge or slide past one
another, the crust is stressed and fractured, and magma generated at depth finds a route
to the surface.

## Divergent and Convergent Settings

At a divergent boundary two plates pull apart and the underlying mantle rises to fill
the gap. As it rises the pressure on it falls, which lowers its melting point and
produces basaltic magma without any additional heat being supplied, a process called
decompression melting. The magma erupts along the rift to build new oceanic crust. This
mechanism operates continuously along mid-ocean ridges such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge,
and it surfaces on land in Iceland and in the East African Rift.

At a convergent boundary an oceanic plate is forced beneath a neighbouring plate in a
process called subduction. Water carried down with the descending slab is released into
the overlying mantle, lowering its melting point and generating magma that is richer in
silica than mid-ocean ridge basalt. Silica-rich magma is far more viscous, so gas
struggles to escape and pressure accumulates until it is released explosively. The arc
of volcanoes above a subduction zone, such as the Cascades or the Andes, is therefore
associated with the most violent eruptions. A third setting, the hotspot, occurs away
from plate margins where a plume of unusually hot mantle burns through the moving plate,
producing chains such as the Hawaiian Islands.

## Eruption Styles and Volcanic Hazards

Magma chemistry largely determines how a volcano behaves. Low-viscosity basaltic magma
allows gas to bubble out freely, so eruptions are effusive and produce broad, gently
sloping shield volcanoes built from successive lava flows. High-viscosity andesitic and
rhyolitic magma traps gas, and the resulting explosive eruptions build steep-sided
composite cones from alternating layers of lava and fragmented rock. Volcanologists
grade explosivity on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, a logarithmic scale from zero to
eight based mainly on the volume of material ejected.

The hazards differ accordingly. Lava flows destroy property but move slowly enough that
people can usually walk clear. The lethal hazards are pyroclastic flows, avalanches of
superheated gas and ash that travel at hundreds of kilometres an hour, and lahars, dense
mudflows formed when ash mixes with rain or melting snow and which can travel far down
valleys long after an eruption ends. Ash clouds collapse roofs, contaminate water and
ground aircraft, and the sulphur dioxide injected into the stratosphere by very large
eruptions can cool global temperatures measurably for a year or more.

## Monitoring and Prediction

Volcanoes rarely erupt without warning, and monitoring networks look for several
independent signs that magma is moving. Swarms of small earthquakes trace the fracturing
of rock as magma forces a path upward. Ground deformation measured by tiltmeters, GPS
receivers and satellite radar interferometry reveals the swelling of a magma reservoir.
Changes in the ratio of gases emitted from fumaroles, particularly a rise in sulphur
dioxide relative to water vapour, can indicate that fresh magma has arrived at shallow
depth.

Forecasting remains probabilistic rather than precise. Scientists can often say that an
eruption is likely within days or weeks, but the exact timing and size stay uncertain,
which makes evacuation decisions difficult and politically fraught. Hazard maps built
from the deposits of past eruptions guide land-use planning, and staged alert levels
give civil authorities a common language for escalating a response. The eruption of
Mount Pinatubo in 1991 is the standard example of monitoring working well: recognition
of the precursors led to an evacuation that saved many thousands of lives.
