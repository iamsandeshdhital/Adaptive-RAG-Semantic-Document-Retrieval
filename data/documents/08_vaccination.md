## Overview

Vaccination provokes the immune system into responding to a harmless version or fragment
of a pathogen so that a later encounter with the real organism is met by a defence that
is already prepared. The practice grew out of the observation, made by Edward Jenner in
1796, that dairy workers who had contracted cowpox appeared to be protected against
smallpox. Smallpox is the only human disease so far eradicated, declared eliminated in
1980 after a global vaccination campaign.

The immune system has two arms. Innate immunity is present from birth, acts within
minutes to hours and responds in the same way regardless of the invader, relying on
barriers, phagocytes and inflammation. Adaptive immunity is slower to start but specific
to a particular antigen and, critically, it remembers. Vaccination is possible only
because of that memory.

## The Primary and Secondary Response

An antigen is any molecule the immune system can recognise as foreign, usually a protein
or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen. When a B lymphocyte with a matching
receptor encounters its antigen, and receives confirming signals from a helper T cell,
it divides repeatedly in a process called clonal expansion. Most of the daughter cells
become plasma cells that secrete large quantities of antibody; the remainder become
memory cells that persist long after the infection is cleared.

This first encounter is the primary response, and it is slow, typically taking one to two
weeks to reach a useful antibody concentration, which is why a first infection can make
a person seriously ill. On re-exposure, the pool of memory cells is far larger than the
original population of naive cells and responds without needing to be primed again. The
secondary response therefore produces more antibody, produces it within days rather than
weeks, and produces antibody of higher affinity because the memory cells have been
refined by affinity maturation. The pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms appear.

## Types of Vaccine

Live attenuated vaccines use a weakened strain that replicates enough to provoke a
strong, durable response but not enough to cause disease; measles, mumps and rubella are
delivered this way. Because the organism replicates, protection is often lifelong after
one or two doses, but the vaccine is unsuitable for people with compromised immunity and
usually needs refrigeration. Inactivated vaccines use killed organisms and are more
stable and safer for immunocompromised patients, but they provoke a weaker response and
generally require booster doses.

Subunit, conjugate and toxoid vaccines present only part of the pathogen. A subunit
vaccine uses a purified surface protein; a conjugate vaccine links a bacterial
polysaccharide to a carrier protein so that young children, who respond poorly to
polysaccharide alone, mount a proper response; a toxoid vaccine uses an inactivated
bacterial toxin, as for tetanus and diphtheria. Newer platforms deliver genetic
instructions instead of protein. A messenger RNA vaccine encloses mRNA coding for a
pathogen protein in lipid nanoparticles, and the recipient cells manufacture the antigen
themselves. Because only the sequence needs to change to target a new pathogen, these
platforms can be redesigned quickly. Many vaccines also contain an adjuvant, a substance
that amplifies the immune response to the antigen without being an antigen itself.

## Herd Immunity and Programme Design

When a sufficiently large fraction of a population is immune, each infected person
transmits to fewer than one other on average and sustained transmission collapses. This
indirect protection, called herd immunity, shields people who cannot be vaccinated, such
as newborns and patients receiving immunosuppressive treatment. The threshold depends on
how transmissible the disease is: it is estimated at around ninety-five per cent for
measles, which spreads exceptionally efficiently, and lower for less contagious
diseases.

Programme design must contend with waning immunity and with pathogen change. Antibody
titres fall over time for some vaccines, so boosters restore protection by re-stimulating
memory cells. Influenza vaccines must be reformulated annually because the virus
accumulates surface mutations, a process called antigenic drift, and occasionally
undergoes a larger reassortment known as antigenic shift that can produce a pandemic
strain. Surveillance of circulating strains, cold chain logistics and public confidence
all determine whether a technically effective vaccine translates into a reduction in
disease.
