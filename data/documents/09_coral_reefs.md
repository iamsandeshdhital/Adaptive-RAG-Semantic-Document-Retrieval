## Overview

A coral reef is a structure built from the calcium carbonate skeletons of colonial
animals called corals. Reefs occupy well under one per cent of the ocean floor yet
support an estimated quarter of all marine species, which makes them the most
biologically concentrated habitat in the sea. They also protect coastlines by dissipating
wave energy before it reaches the shore, and they underpin fisheries and tourism for
hundreds of millions of people.

Reef-building corals are restricted to a narrow set of conditions. They require clear,
shallow, nutrient-poor water, typically less than fifty metres deep so that enough light
penetrates, and temperatures broadly between twenty-three and twenty-nine degrees
Celsius. This confines reefs mainly to tropical and subtropical seas. The apparent
paradox of extraordinary productivity in nutrient-poor water is explained by very
efficient recycling of nutrients within the reef community.

## The Coral and Algal Partnership

Each coral polyp is a small animal resembling a sea anemone, with a ring of stinging
tentacles around a central mouth, that secretes a hard skeleton of aragonite beneath
itself. Colonies grow as polyps divide, and successive generations build on the skeletons
of their predecessors, so a reef is largely a mineral deposit produced by living tissue
occupying only its surface.

The partnership at the centre of reef biology is between the polyp and single-celled
algae called zooxanthellae that live inside its tissues. The algae photosynthesise and
transfer sugars to the host, in some cases supplying the great majority of its energy
budget; in return they receive shelter and a reliable supply of the nitrogen and
phosphorus compounds released by the animal. This mutualistic symbiosis is what allows
corals to thrive where dissolved nutrients are scarce, and it is also why light
availability sets the depth limit of a reef. The zooxanthellae give corals most of their
colour.

## Bleaching and Thermal Stress

When corals are stressed, most commonly by water a degree or two above the usual summer
maximum sustained for several weeks, they expel their zooxanthellae. The tissue becomes
transparent and the white skeleton shows through, which is why the phenomenon is called
bleaching. Bleaching is not death; if conditions return to normal quickly the algae can
be reacquired. If the stress persists the colony starves, because it has lost the
photosynthetic partner that supplied its energy.

Mass bleaching events driven by marine heatwaves have become markedly more frequent, and
the interval between them is now often shorter than the decade or more that a damaged
reef needs to recover its structure. Bleaching interacts with other pressures. Nutrient
runoff from agriculture favours fast-growing macroalgae that outcompete coral recruits,
sediment from coastal development reduces the light reaching the algae, destructive
fishing removes the herbivorous fish that keep algae in check, and outbreaks of predators
such as the crown-of-thorns starfish strip live tissue directly.

## Acidification and the Carbonate System

The ocean has absorbed a large share of the carbon dioxide released by human activity.
Dissolved carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates and
releases hydrogen ions, lowering pH. Surface ocean pH has fallen by about zero point one
units since the industrial revolution, which because the scale is logarithmic represents
an increase in hydrogen ion concentration of roughly thirty per cent. This process is
ocean acidification, and it is distinct from warming although driven by the same
emissions.

The consequence for reefs runs through the carbonate chemistry. The additional hydrogen
ions combine with carbonate ions to form hydrogencarbonate, reducing the concentration of
the carbonate that corals need in order to deposit aragonite. The relevant measure is
the aragonite saturation state, and as it declines, calcification slows and skeletons
become less dense, while dissolution of existing carbonate structure accelerates. Warming
and acidification therefore attack a reef from two directions at once, one destroying the
symbiosis that powers growth and the other raising the cost of building. Management
responses such as marine protected areas, catchment controls on runoff, coral gardening
and the selection of heat-tolerant genotypes can improve local resilience, but none
address the underlying chemistry, which depends on global emissions.
