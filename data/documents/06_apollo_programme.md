## Overview

The Apollo programme was the United States effort to land humans on the Moon and return
them safely to Earth. It was announced by President John F. Kennedy in a speech to
Congress in May 1961, which set the goal of achieving the landing before the end of the
decade. The commitment followed a series of Soviet firsts in spaceflight, and the
programme was as much an instrument of Cold War competition as a scientific venture. At
its peak Apollo employed around four hundred thousand people across government,
industry and universities.

Apollo was preceded by two smaller programmes that supplied the necessary experience.
Project Mercury demonstrated that a human could survive launch, orbit and re-entry.
Project Gemini developed the techniques Apollo would depend on: long-duration flight,
spacewalking, and above all rendezvous and docking between two craft in orbit. Without
the Gemini docking experience the mission architecture eventually chosen for Apollo
would not have been credible.

## Mission Architecture

Planners considered three routes to the Moon. Direct ascent would have sent a single
enormous vehicle all the way to the surface and back. Earth orbit rendezvous would have
assembled a lunar vehicle in orbit around the Earth from several launches. The option
adopted was lunar orbit rendezvous, in which one launch sends a combined spacecraft to
the Moon, a small dedicated lander descends to the surface while the main craft waits in
lunar orbit, and the lander returns to dock before the journey home. Lunar orbit
rendezvous was the lightest and cheapest option but also the least forgiving, since a
failed docking in lunar orbit left no rescue possibility.

The spacecraft had three parts. The command module carried the crew of three and was the
only component designed to survive re-entry. The service module supplied propulsion,
electrical power from fuel cells, oxygen and water. The lunar module, carried folded
beneath the other two during the outbound flight, had a descent stage with a throttleable
engine and landing legs and an ascent stage that returned two astronauts to orbit. The
launch vehicle was the Saturn V, a three-stage rocket standing about one hundred and ten
metres tall whose five first-stage F-1 engines burned kerosene and liquid oxygen.

## Flights and Setbacks

The programme suffered an early disaster. In January 1967 a fire broke out during a
ground test of Apollo 1 in a cabin pressurised with pure oxygen, killing astronauts Gus
Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. The investigation forced a redesign of the hatch,
the wiring and the choice of cabin materials, and the resulting engineering discipline is
widely credited with the reliability of later flights. Apollo 8 then carried the first
crew to orbit the Moon at Christmas 1968, and Apollo 9 and 10 rehearsed the lunar module
in Earth orbit and lunar orbit respectively.

Apollo 11 landed on 20 July 1969, with Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin descending to the
Sea of Tranquillity while Michael Collins remained in the command module. Apollo 13,
launched in April 1970, was aborted when an oxygen tank in the service module ruptured
on the way out; the crew used the lunar module as a lifeboat and returned alive, an
episode remembered as a successful failure. Six missions landed in total, the last being
Apollo 17 in December 1972, and the later flights carried a wheeled lunar roving vehicle
that greatly extended the area a crew could explore.

## Scientific Results and Legacy

The missions returned about three hundred and eighty kilograms of rock and soil. Analysis
of these samples showed that lunar and terrestrial oxygen isotopes match closely, which
became the central evidence for the giant impact hypothesis, the idea that the Moon
formed from debris thrown off when a body roughly the size of Mars struck the early
Earth. Radiometric dating of the samples established a chronology for the lunar surface
that is still used to estimate the age of cratered surfaces elsewhere in the solar
system. Instruments left behind, including seismometers and laser retroreflectors,
continued to return data for years, and the retroreflectors are still used to measure the
distance to the Moon.

Apollo was cancelled early; three planned landings were dropped as public interest and
budgets fell. Its technical legacy lies less in any single invention than in the
management of extreme complexity, including the development of integrated circuits for
the Apollo Guidance Computer and the systems engineering practices adopted across
aerospace afterwards. No crewed mission has returned to the lunar surface since 1972.
