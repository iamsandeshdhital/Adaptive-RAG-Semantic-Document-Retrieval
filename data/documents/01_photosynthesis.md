## Overview

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria convert light
energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. The overall reaction consumes six
molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water for every molecule of glucose
produced, releasing six molecules of oxygen as a by-product. Because almost every food
chain on the planet begins with a photosynthetic organism, the process underpins nearly
all life on Earth, and the oxygen it releases maintains the composition of the
atmosphere that aerobic organisms depend on.

The reaction takes place inside organelles called chloroplasts. A chloroplast is
enclosed by a double membrane and contains a dense internal membrane system arranged
into flattened sacs known as thylakoids. Stacks of thylakoids are called grana, and the
fluid that surrounds them is the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll sits within the
thylakoid membranes, where it absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red parts of
the visible spectrum and reflects green light, which is why most foliage appears green.

## The Light-Dependent Reactions

The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. When a chlorophyll
molecule in photosystem II absorbs a photon, one of its electrons is raised to a higher
energy level and is passed to an electron transport chain. The electron lost by
photosystem II is replaced by splitting a water molecule, a step called photolysis that
releases oxygen gas and hydrogen ions. As electrons travel down the transport chain,
the energy released is used to pump hydrogen ions into the thylakoid space, building a
concentration gradient across the membrane.

Hydrogen ions then flow back into the stroma through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and
the movement drives the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate. This mechanism, known as
chemiosmosis, is the same principle that mitochondria use during respiration. At
photosystem I the electrons are re-energised by a second photon and finally reduce the
carrier molecule NADP+ to NADPH. The light-dependent stage therefore produces three
things the plant needs: ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

## The Calvin Cycle

The light-independent reactions, usually called the Calvin cycle, take place in the
stroma and do not require light directly, although they depend on the ATP and NADPH
supplied by the light-dependent stage. Carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf through
pores called stomata and is fixed by the enzyme ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase,
commonly abbreviated to RuBisCO. RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon
acceptor molecule, ribulose bisphosphate, producing an unstable six-carbon intermediate
that immediately splits into two three-carbon molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate.

These three-carbon molecules are reduced using NADPH and ATP to form triose phosphate.
Most of the triose phosphate is recycled to regenerate ribulose bisphosphate so the
cycle can continue, while the remainder is exported and combined into glucose, sucrose,
starch or cellulose. RuBisCO is thought to be the most abundant enzyme on Earth, yet it
is comparatively slow and can mistakenly bind oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a
wasteful side reaction called photorespiration.

## Limiting Factors

The rate of photosynthesis is governed by whichever environmental factor is in shortest
supply. Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature are the three
principal limiting factors. At low light intensity the rate increases roughly in
proportion to the available light, then levels off once the light-dependent reactions
are saturated. Raising temperature accelerates enzyme activity up to an optimum,
typically around twenty-five to thirty degrees Celsius for temperate plants, beyond
which enzymes such as RuBisCO begin to denature and the rate falls sharply.

Water availability matters indirectly. When a plant loses too much water it closes its
stomata to conserve moisture, which also blocks the entry of carbon dioxide and starves
the Calvin cycle of its raw material. Some plants have evolved around this constraint.
Maize and sugarcane use the C4 pathway, which concentrates carbon dioxide around
RuBisCO before fixation, while desert succulents use crassulacean acid metabolism to
collect carbon dioxide at night and process it during the day. Commercial growers apply
the same logic in reverse, enriching glasshouses with carbon dioxide and supplementing
light to push growth beyond what field conditions allow.
