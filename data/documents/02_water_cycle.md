## Overview

The water cycle, also called the hydrological cycle, describes the continuous movement
of water between the oceans, the atmosphere, the land surface and the subsurface. Water
is neither created nor destroyed in the process; it simply changes state and location.
The cycle is driven almost entirely by energy from the Sun, which evaporates surface
water, and by gravity, which returns it to the sea. Roughly five hundred thousand cubic
kilometres of water evaporate from the Earth's surface each year, the great majority of
it from the ocean.

Although the planet is covered by water, only a small fraction is usable freshwater.
About ninety-seven per cent of the world's water is saline ocean water. Of the remaining
three per cent, roughly two thirds is locked in glaciers and polar ice caps, and most of
the rest sits underground as groundwater. Rivers and lakes, the sources humans draw on
most easily, hold well under one per cent of the total. This uneven distribution explains
why water scarcity can occur on a planet that is mostly water.

## Evaporation, Transpiration and Condensation

Evaporation converts liquid water at the surface into water vapour. The rate depends on
temperature, humidity, wind speed and the surface area exposed. Plants contribute a
parallel flux called transpiration, drawing water up from the soil through their roots
and releasing vapour through the stomata of their leaves. Because the two processes are
difficult to measure separately in the field, hydrologists often combine them into a
single term, evapotranspiration, which in heavily vegetated catchments can exceed direct
evaporation from open water.

Rising air expands and cools as atmospheric pressure falls with altitude. Once the air
reaches its dew point it becomes saturated, and vapour condenses onto microscopic
particles of dust, salt or pollution known as condensation nuclei. The resulting droplets
are far too small to fall, so they remain suspended as cloud. Droplets grow by colliding
and coalescing, and in colder clouds by the deposition of vapour onto ice crystals,
until they become heavy enough for gravity to overcome the updraught.

## Precipitation and Runoff

Precipitation returns water to the surface as rain, snow, sleet, hail or drizzle.
Meteorologists classify it by the mechanism that lifted the air. Convectional rainfall
follows the strong heating of a land surface and produces the towering cumulonimbus
clouds associated with thunderstorms. Orographic rainfall occurs when prevailing winds
are forced to rise over a mountain barrier, which is why windward slopes are wet and the
leeward side lies in a comparatively dry rain shadow. Frontal rainfall develops where a
warm air mass is undercut by a colder one along a weather front.

Water that reaches the ground follows several paths. Some evaporates immediately, some
infiltrates the soil, and the remainder travels overland as surface runoff into streams
and rivers. Infiltrated water may move sideways through the soil as throughflow or
percolate deeper until it reaches the water table and joins an aquifer. Groundwater
moves slowly, sometimes only a few metres a year, and can remain in storage for
centuries before emerging at a spring or seeping into a riverbed. The average residence
time of a water molecule in the atmosphere, by contrast, is only about nine days.

## Human Influence and Catchment Management

Human activity alters almost every part of the cycle. Sealing land with roads and
buildings replaces permeable soil with impermeable surfaces, cutting infiltration and
sending a greater share of rainfall straight into drains, which raises flood peaks
downstream. Deforestation reduces transpiration and interception, while irrigation
transfers vast quantities of groundwater to the atmosphere. Abstracting groundwater
faster than it is recharged lowers the water table permanently, and in coastal aquifers
it can draw saltwater inland.

Catchment management tries to restore balance rather than simply build higher defences.
Restoring wetlands and floodplains provides temporary storage that flattens flood peaks,
and planting vegetation along riverbanks slows runoff and traps sediment. Because a
warmer atmosphere holds roughly seven per cent more moisture for every degree of
warming, climate change is expected to intensify the cycle, bringing heavier downpours
in some regions and longer droughts in others even where total annual rainfall changes
little.
