## Overview

The circulatory system transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, heat and waste products
around the body. It consists of the heart, which acts as a pump, a network of blood
vessels, and the blood itself. In humans the system is described as closed, because the
blood remains inside vessels, and as a double circulation, because blood passes through
the heart twice on each complete circuit.

The double arrangement separates two loops. In the pulmonary circulation, deoxygenated
blood is pumped from the right side of the heart to the lungs and returns oxygenated to
the left side. In the systemic circulation, the left side pumps that oxygenated blood to
the rest of the body and receives it back deoxygenated. Keeping the loops separate
allows the systemic circuit to run at high pressure for efficient delivery while the
delicate pulmonary capillaries are supplied at low pressure.

## The Heart and the Cardiac Cycle

The heart has four chambers: two thin-walled atria that receive blood and two
thick-walled ventricles that pump it out. The left ventricle has a considerably thicker
muscular wall than the right because it must generate enough pressure to drive blood
around the entire body, whereas the right ventricle only has to reach the lungs. Valves
enforce one-way flow. The atrioventricular valves sit between each atrium and ventricle,
and the semilunar valves guard the exits into the aorta and the pulmonary artery.

The cardiac cycle has three stages. During atrial systole the atria contract and top up
the ventricles. During ventricular systole the ventricles contract, pressure rises and
forces the atrioventricular valves shut, producing the first heart sound, and blood is
ejected through the semilunar valves. During diastole the muscle relaxes, pressure falls
below that in the arteries, and the semilunar valves snap closed, giving the second
heart sound. The rhythm is set by the sinoatrial node in the wall of the right atrium, a
patch of tissue that depolarises spontaneously and is therefore called the pacemaker.
The impulse is delayed briefly at the atrioventricular node, allowing the atria to
finish emptying before the ventricles contract.

## Blood Vessels

Arteries carry blood away from the heart. They have narrow lumens and thick walls
containing elastic fibres and smooth muscle, which stretch during systole and recoil
during diastole, smoothing the pulsatile output into steadier flow. Arteries branch into
arterioles, whose muscular walls can constrict or dilate to redirect blood towards the
tissues that need it most, which is how blood is shifted to skeletal muscle during
exercise and to the skin during heat loss.

Capillaries are the site of exchange. Their walls are a single endothelial cell thick
and are permeable, and their combined cross-sectional area is so large that blood slows
to a crawl, giving time for oxygen, glucose and carbon dioxide to diffuse. No cell in
the body lies more than a fraction of a millimetre from a capillary. Blood then collects
into venules and veins, which have wide lumens, thin walls and one-way valves. Because
venous pressure is low, return to the heart depends heavily on the squeezing action of
surrounding skeletal muscle and on pressure changes during breathing.

## Blood and Its Functions

Blood is a tissue made of plasma and suspended cells. Plasma is mostly water and carries
dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts, hormones, plasma proteins and most of the
transported carbon dioxide as hydrogencarbonate ions. Red blood cells, which lose their
nucleus during development to make room for haemoglobin, carry oxygen. Haemoglobin binds
oxygen readily where the partial pressure is high, in the lungs, and releases it where
the partial pressure is low, in respiring tissue, a relationship described by the
sigmoid oxygen dissociation curve.

White blood cells defend the body. Phagocytes engulf pathogens, while lymphocytes
produce antibodies and coordinate the immune response. Platelets are cell fragments that
trigger clotting, adhering to damaged vessel walls and initiating a cascade that
converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads. Disease often reflects the
failure of one of these components. Atherosclerosis narrows arteries with fatty plaques
and raises the risk of a clot blocking the coronary arteries that supply the heart
muscle itself, causing a myocardial infarction.
