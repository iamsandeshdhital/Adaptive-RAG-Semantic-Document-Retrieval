## Overview

An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or prevents them from multiplying.
Antibiotics act only on bacteria; they have no effect on viruses, which is why they
cannot treat influenza or the common cold. The modern era began in 1928 when Alexander
Fleming noticed that a mould contaminating one of his culture plates had cleared the
surrounding bacterial growth. The active substance was penicillin, and its purification
in the early 1940s by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain turned a laboratory curiosity into a
medicine that transformed the treatment of infection.

Drugs are described as bactericidal if they kill bacteria outright and bacteriostatic if
they merely halt reproduction and leave the immune system to finish the job.
Antibiotics are also classed by their spectrum. A narrow-spectrum drug targets a limited
range of species and spares much of the normal gut flora, while a broad-spectrum drug
acts against many species and is useful when the causative organism has not yet been
identified, at the cost of greater disruption to the microbiome.

## Mechanisms of Action

Useful antibiotics exploit features of bacterial cells that human cells lack, a property
called selective toxicity. The penicillins and cephalosporins, together known as the
beta-lactams, block the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell
wall. Without cross-linking the wall cannot withstand internal osmotic pressure, and the
cell bursts. Human cells have no cell wall, so the drug is comparatively safe.

Other classes attack different targets. Aminoglycosides and tetracyclines bind to the
bacterial ribosome and disrupt protein synthesis, taking advantage of the structural
differences between bacterial and human ribosomes. The quinolones inhibit DNA gyrase,
the enzyme that relieves the strain of unwinding the chromosome during replication.
Sulfonamides interrupt the synthesis of folate, a vitamin that bacteria must manufacture
themselves but that humans absorb from the diet.

## How Resistance Arises

Resistance is evolution in action. Within any large bacterial population, chance
mutations produce a few individuals that survive exposure to a drug. When the antibiotic
is applied, susceptible cells die and the survivors reproduce, so the resistant variant
comes to dominate. Because bacteria divide rapidly, this shift can occur within days.
The drug does not cause the mutation; it selects for one that was already present.

Bacteria also acquire resistance ready-made. Genes conferring resistance often sit on
plasmids, small circular pieces of DNA that can be transferred between cells during
conjugation, sometimes between different species. This horizontal gene transfer allows
resistance to spread far faster than mutation alone would permit, and it explains how
plasmids carrying several resistance genes at once give rise to multidrug-resistant
strains. At the biochemical level the mechanisms include enzymes such as beta-lactamase
that destroy the drug, changes to the target protein that reduce binding, reduced
membrane permeability, and efflux pumps that expel the drug before it can act.

## Stewardship and the Clinical Response

Resistance is accelerated by how antibiotics are used. Prescribing them for viral
illness, stopping a course early, and their routine use as growth promoters in
agriculture all expose bacterial populations to selective pressure without clinical
benefit. Antimicrobial stewardship programmes therefore aim to prescribe only when a
bacterial infection is likely, to narrow the spectrum once laboratory culture and
sensitivity testing identify the organism, and to keep certain agents in reserve.

Where resistance is already established, clinicians combine drugs so that a single
mutation is unlikely to defeat the whole regimen, which is standard practice in
tuberculosis treatment. Beta-lactams are often paired with a beta-lactamase inhibitor
such as clavulanic acid to protect the active drug from enzymatic attack. Infection
control measures, particularly hand hygiene, isolation of colonised patients and
vaccination that removes the need for treatment in the first place, reduce transmission
of resistant strains. The pipeline of genuinely new antibiotic classes has been thin for
decades, partly because a drug intended to be used sparingly is commercially
unattractive, so conserving the effectiveness of existing agents remains the main
defence.
